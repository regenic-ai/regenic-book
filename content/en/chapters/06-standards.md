# Chapter 6: No Standard, Stop Execution

Status: Completed (v2.0, 2026-09-21; rewritten from the Chinese authority in English voice; open to ongoing public revision)

**Claim: A standard is judgment written as executable, checkable, revisable clauses. When no usable standard is on hand, stop unstandardized scaled execution and turn to finding, borrowing, or verifying a standard.**

---

## 1. Judgment becomes a standard only when it is written down

Money can buy more execution. Time can buy more tries. The organization is still limited by the capacity to produce reliable judgment. Turning one judgment into reusable material keeps the same kind of problem from having to be judged from scratch every time.

A sentence that "this plan cannot ship" leaves only a conclusion. It does not record the object of the trade-off, the evidence, the conditions of use, or the signal that would make it fail. After a handoff, other people can only copy the conclusion. After the environment changes, they also cannot tell from it whether the original conclusion still holds.

What a standard has to keep is what sat before the conclusion: under what conditions this judgment applies, what it requires or forbids, what evidence confirms that the work is good, what situation means it must stop, and which new facts would require a revision. Write those contents into a carrier that everyone involved can read, check, and update, and one judgment begins to leave the person and become reusable organizational material.

Writing it down is only a necessary condition. The clauses still have to be findable and understandable on the floor, and someone has to own execution, leave evidence, and handle exceptions. The door-plug accident at the Renton plant shows that a procedure that has existed for years can still fail to constrain critical work on the floor.

Liuzhou snail noodles supply a lighter contrast. In 2015, Liuzhou began organizing a local food-safety standard. The 2016 implementation of `DBS45/034—2016` wrote requirements for prepackaged product ingredients, physical and chemical indicators, microbial limits, testing, and packaging into clauses that can be checked (People's Daily Online, 2021; DBS45/034—2016). It did not pack "authentic taste" whole into a file, and still less can it, on its own, explain later growth of the whole industry. What it can show is only this: judgments that used to sit scattered in production experience and in inspectors' hands can be written as conditions different firms can check against.

A standard is this: **write down the reusable part of a judgment, so that other people can execute it, check it, and revise it.** It does not replace all experience, and it is not measured by the number of files. It keeps the same kind of judgment from depending on one person being in the room.

## 2. Standards, context, and execution carriers are not the same thing

The materials in a shared folder cannot all be called standards. Judgment standards and context are two different kinds of shared input. One handles the basis of the trade-off; the other handles the present facts.

**A judgment standard answers how to choose.** It says what ranks higher, what counts as good, which boundaries cannot be crossed, and what evidence is enough to support a conclusion. In customer support handling a refund, for example: is the priority a one-and-done resolution, or, when the facts are thin, protection of the account? What amount can be handled directly, and what must be escalated? Which promises must never be made to the customer? Those contents decide how the same set of facts will be evaluated.

**Context answers what the facts in front of you are.** On the same refund request, the order record, the customer history, the current policy version, the evidence already verified, and the assumptions still unconfirmed are all context. They keep changing. Two people using the same standard, who see different versions of the order or the policy, will still give different answers. What then needs repair is the information, not a new ruler invented from scratch.

**A process or workflow answers in what order things happen.** Read the order first, then check the policy, then generate a reply, and hand it to a person if needed: that is a set of steps. A step can embed a judgment standard—"escalate if one item of evidence is missing"—and it can call context—"read the refund policy now in force." The sequence itself is not a standard. A process can run smoothly and still produce bad results, stably, by a wrong standard.

An SOP usually packs all three into one document: background, operating steps, checkpoints, and a qualification line. It is an execution carrier. It is not a fourth kind of shared input, and it is not a synonym for a judgment standard. Before you hand an SOP to another team or to an AI, the first move is not to copy the whole file. It is to take the sentences apart: which lines set the trade-off, which paragraphs supply present facts, and which contents only arrange the steps.

A prompt is a container too. One prompt can hold a task goal, a judgment standard, input material, working steps, and an output format. An evaluation rule that sets priority, evidence requirements, and boundaries belongs to the standard. Attached customer files and policy documents belong to context. "Search first, then draft, then review" belongs to workflow. Requiring a given system, or allowing only a given class of data to be read, involves tools and permissions. They cannot all be called standards merely because they appear in the same stretch of text.

Wishes and experience have their own places. "Write a reply that satisfies the customer" does not define satisfaction, acceptance evidence, or a boundary that cannot be sacrificed. It is a wish. The handling experience a veteran has accumulated can propose a candidate standard. The reusable relations and conditions of use still have to be extracted and verified. The standard says how to judge. Context supplies the facts being judged. The execution carrier brings both into the work.

## 3. What "stop execution" actually stops

"No standard, stop execution" does not mean stopping all action. What has to stop is **continuing to raise the volume, range, and irreversibility of execution while the judgment is still unclear.**

A piece of work should trigger a pause when both of the following hold.

First, it is about to be repeated, widened, or handed off. One reply is about to become ten thousand replies. A store trial is about to be rolled out nationally. A small test spend is about to become a standing budget. A piece of work is about to leave the original decision-maker for a newcomer, an outside team, or an AI. Once execution volume rises, the same patch of vagueness is copied again and again.

Second, it has no usable standard. The qualification line and the stop condition may never have been written. The original standard may have expired, may conflict with another, or may not apply to the new scene. Necessary context may be missing, so execution has no factual basis. Pushing for speed then adds not effective output, but unverified acts of judgment.

So the operable rule is this:

**For work that is repeated, scaled, irreversible, high-cost, or about to be handed to other people and to AI, if no usable standard is on hand, stop expanding execution. Turn the task into finding, borrowing, or verifying a standard.**

After the pause is triggered, the object of the work becomes finding an outside standard, checking the conditions of use, and filling in context. When no mature answer exists, the original task shrinks into a bounded trial. A trial has to state what is being tested, a cap on time and cost, the evidence to be watched, and a stop condition, so that it produces the feedback the next version of the standard needs.

Exploratory work can continue inside a temporary boundary: it affects only a small set of objects, it changes only one key variable, it does not cross a safety or integrity line, and it can be reversed after a miss. Exploration allows the answer to be unknown. It does not allow unbounded cost, and it does not allow results to go unrecorded. Before a standard is formed, a trial cannot convert directly into full execution.

Emergencies are another boundary. When there is a fire, a data leak, a risk to people, or a cash chain that is failing, you do not delay stopping the damage in order to complete a document. The organization should act inside a pre-authorized safety boundary, first contain the harm that is still spreading, then add the record, review, and revise the standard. The pause rule protects the quality of judgment. It does not put procedure above life, safety, and survival.

A person's judgment on the spot does not disappear either. Low-frequency, low-cost, highly specific situations can be left to the person present. Even then, a behavioral red line, an escalation condition, and an after-the-fact record can still be written clearly. Standardization is not writing every answer in advance. It is deciding which parts should no longer be guessed.

A pause also has to have a response. Any person executing the work can report a gap in standards, but the report cannot dump the task into a queue nobody owns. The owner has to give, inside an agreed time, one of three clear treatments: an existing standard applies; the work enters a bounded trial; or it is escalated to someone with the authority to bear the risk. A pause is not an endless wait. It turns "keep going" into a decision that needs a reason.

## 4. Boeing: a procedure on paper, no usable standard on the floor

On January 5, 2024, about six minutes after Alaska Airlines Flight 1282 left Portland, a left mid-exit door plug separated from the fuselage at about 14,830 feet, and the cabin rapidly lost pressure. One flight attendant and seven passengers were slightly injured. The airplane was substantially damaged. The National Transportation Safety Board later confirmed that the four bolts that retained the door plug, and that kept it from moving upward, had already been missing before the new airplane was delivered to the airline (NTSB, 2025).

Boeing had a clear procedure. While the airplane was being assembled at the Renton plant, five rivets ahead of the door plug needed rework. To reach the rivets, manufacturing personnel had to open the door plug, and opening it necessarily meant removing the four retaining bolts and associated hardware. Boeing's Business Process Instruction (BPI) at the time was explicit: whenever an already accepted installation was disturbed, a removal record had to be generated first.

The removal record was supposed to leave, in the manufacturing system, the status "this accepted installation has been taken apart," to list the actions to restore it, the sign-off steps for manufacturing and quality, and a final quality check. It tied the trigger, the reinstallation actions, and acceptance responsibility together, so that later people would not have to rely on memory to confirm that the four bolts had gone back.

That trigger had already entered the floor record. On September 17, 2023, Boeing's on-site coordination record stated that if removal was needed, a removal record had to be written first. The manager coordinating the work later said that no door-team member with the relevant capability was available, and that the work should wait until the right people arrived.

The next morning, though, the record became "door being opened by mechanics." The investigation found no corresponding removal record, and nobody could say who had opened the door plug. After the rivet rework was finished, the door plug was closed during the second shift on September 19. No door-team member was on duty then, and the people interviewed likewise did not know who had completed the final close. The four retaining bolts were not reinstalled, and the close was not given a quality check. Interior and insulation work then continued, and the missing bolts were covered.

A stop signal had already appeared. A handling opinion—wait for the right people—had already been formed. Production still continued. After the removal record was missing, later systems showed only that "the rivet rework has been handled." They did not show that "the door plug has not been restored." Quality personnel checked the problems already recorded in the system. They did not check a problem that had never entered the record. The production process therefore kept moving forward.

The NTSB found that the BPI then used to decide whether a removal record was needed ran to more than fifty pages, contained multiple links to other instructions, and listed more "no record needed" exceptions than positive guidance on "when a record is needed." Boeing managers confirmed that the instruction had grown more complex and harder to use. Related on-the-job training had no structure and no complete record, and it mainly covered daily assembly tasks. Opening a door plug was a rare, non-routine task, and the most experienced people were not present at the time.

This removal process already had a record of compliance problems for at least ten years before the accident. Boeing had made document revisions, quality alerts, training, and other corrective actions, and the problems continued. The final report's probable cause was that Boeing did not provide adequate training, guidance, and oversight so that manufacturing personnel could consistently and correctly follow the removal process. The Federal Aviation Administration's failure to identify the recurring systemic noncompliance and to drive Boeing to fix it was a contributing factor (NTSB, 2025).

The NTSB's conclusion points to a system problem, not a single worker's miss or the page count of a file. The critical breaks were five: the clauses were not clear enough to use; the trigger did not actually stop the work; the work continued with no one accountable; the key state was not recorded; and the problem kept recurring, while revisions still did not remove the root cause.

A procedure on paper is not a usable standard on the floor. An effective standard has to be findable and understandable, has to put the action in a named person's hands, has to require checkable evidence, and has to send exceptions into revision. Otherwise the file only proves what the organization once wrote. It does not prove that the work was constrained by it.

## 5. A usable standard answers at least five questions

Boeing had documents, a manufacturing system, and quality roles, and critical work was still not constrained by the procedure. A standard about to be issued directly to people or to AI has to answer at least five questions.

1. **Conditions of use: when it applies.** Write the object, the scene, the premises, and the necessary context. After the conditions change, the standard cannot keep its authority on the old title alone. A refund rule has to say which region and which policy version it covers. A manufacturing rule has to say which kind of installation is being disturbed. A standard with no conditions will be moved to places where it should not be used.
2. **Action: who is required to do what.** A standard can require, forbid, give an order of trade-offs, or give an escalation path. The subject cannot be dropped. "Needs to be checked" is not enough. It has to be known whether the person doing the work self-checks, a quality person signs, or an owner approves.
3. **Acceptance evidence: what counts as done.** The result cannot rest only on the person doing the work saying "I did it." Evidence may be a test result, a system record, an independent review, or an observable change on the customer's side. Where the evidence comes from, and where it is kept, also have to be written, or a review later has only memory.
4. **Stop condition: when work cannot continue.** Missing necessary material, a result that crosses a risk line, qualified people not present, or a key assumption overturned can all trigger a stop or an escalation. A standard with no stop condition only urges people forward. It will not stop an error from spreading.
5. **Revision trigger: what makes the standard fail.** A new regulation, a new technology, a new class of exception, a run of deviations, or an agreed review date should all send the standard back to judgment. A standard is not permanently correct because it was written into a system. It is usable under present evidence and present conditions.

The five items do not require one uniform form. They do have to be findable. For repeated work issued directly, missing one item can leave an undefined handling space. "Raise customer satisfaction" has no action and no evidence. "Handle per current policy" has no version and no stop condition. "Find a supervisor when something is abnormal" has no definition of abnormal and no named owner.

In the door-plug accident, the condition of use had already appeared: an accepted installation was about to be opened. The action of generating a removal record already existed. The stop signal did not block the work, the record and the quality sign-off did not form acceptance evidence, and recurring compliance problems did not produce an effective revision. These five items are the minimum questions for checking whether a standard has actually entered the work.

Outside the five, attach an owner, a version, and an effective time. The owner maintains it; that does not mean only the owner can propose a change. The people executing are closest to the exceptions and should be able to feed them back at any time. Version lets the organization know which rule a result was based on at the time. Effective time keeps old and new standards from circulating together. Without those basics, a team can write all five items and still be using different versions.

Mission and belief at the highest layer will not look like a manufacturing checklist. They count as organizational standards only when they can constrain choices below, say who wins in a conflict, and accept regular review—rather than remaining a declaration. The closer to concrete execution, the more complete the five items need to be. The closer to long-term direction, the more you have to be able to see how it changes the trade-offs below.

## 6. How the standards cycle starts to run in one team

Finishing a standard only records the judgment of that moment. Changes in reality, and feedback from execution, will expose missing boundaries. A standard has to return with the results and keep being revised:

**Judgment → Distill → Form the standard → Issue → Execute → Return → Iterate.**

![The standards cycle: judgment, distill, form the standard, issue, execute, return, and iterate](/figures/ch06-standards-machine-en.svg)

*Figure: before issuing, check whether the standard is usable. When a standard is missing, stop expanding execution and turn to finding, borrowing, or bounded verification.*

**Judgment** happens in a concrete choice. Besides the answer, the standard and the context used at the time have to be left behind, or later people cannot tell which ruler the conclusion came from, or which facts it rested on.

**Distill** finds the reusable relation in one decision: which conditions belonged only to that moment, which trade-offs will still hold next time, and what counterexample would overturn it. It keeps the organization from writing a lucky success as a general rule.

**Form the standard** writes the reusable judgment as the five items, and marks an owner, a version, and an effective time. Conditions of use, evidence, and the stop line can then be checked directly.

**Issue** is not a mass notification. It is confirmation that the people executing can find the standard, understand it, and obtain the matching context. When the work is handed to an AI, workflow and tool permissions still have to be loaded into the task separately. A knowledge-base file that has not entered the work's entrance has not been issued.

**Execute** has to produce a result and leave execution evidence. Results that meet the standard need a record. Manual handling that departs from the standard needs a record even more. Once exceptions, reasons, and treatments enter a fixed record, they can become raw material for the next version.

**Return** sends results, failures, exceptions, customer feedback, and risk signals back to the owner. What comes back first is context: what happened, under which conditions, and with what result. The owner then uses those new facts to check the original judgment. Calling execution data a "new standard" mixes the ruler with the facts again.

**Iterate** requires the owner to state why the change was made, what changed, when it takes effect, and how the old version exits, and to notify the execution entrances that are affected. After the new version is issued again, the cycle enters the next round. A standard that facts have already overturned cannot keep being used.

The seven moves do not happen on their own. They still need four operating conditions. First, the team agrees on triggers: repeated rework, a material exception, a risk event, a change in key context, and a piece of work about to be handed to more people or to AI should all start a distillation or a review. Second, each standard or domain of standards has a named owner. "Everyone maintains it" cannot become nobody maintains it. Third, execution feedback enters a fixed place, rather than scattering through meetings and private chats. Fourth, after a revision is triggered, someone publishes the new version and closes the old entrance.

Those four also constrain the stop. After an executor reports a gap, the owner has to judge, inside an agreed time: an existing standard can still be used, the standard needs revision, or the task should shrink into a trial. A stop with no owner and no handling deadline degrades into a new approval bottleneck. A cycle with no stop entrance lets a bad standard run until the result cannot be undone.

The standards cycle does not make the organization forever right. It gives judgment a source, gives execution evidence, and lets error return to the original standard. Personal judgment thereby becomes material a team can use in common and keep revising.

## 7. Which judgments to write first, and which to leave with people

The standards cycle consumes judgment too. Writing every choice as a long document raises maintenance cost. Limited capacity to distill should go first to judgments with higher reuse value.

The first batch usually has several features: the same kind of judgment keeps recurring; one error would cause a high loss; the work has to be handed across people, teams, or AI; the result must be auditable; the conditions of use are relatively stable; and observable feedback is still available after execution. The process with the most rework, a customer trade-off that keeps repeating, a quality check that has to release consistently, and work about to be handed to AI in volume usually sit at the front of that list.

High-frequency, low-risk work may need only a short qualification line, a few exceptions, and an automatic record. Low-frequency, high-risk work may not be able to pre-write a final answer, and should still standardize the material, the evidence, the roles involved, permissions, red lines, and escalation: who can decide, what at least has to be seen, and in what situation work cannot continue.

Low-frequency, low-cost, highly specific judgments can be left to a person's capacity on the spot. Forcing every polite phrasing and every floor adjustment into a standard can cost more to maintain than it returns. The aim of a standard is to cut unnecessary repeated judgment, not to cancel a person's sense of a concrete situation. When a person keeps the final call, the key reason and the result should still be left behind, so the organization can tell a reasonable exception from a new scene the standard missed.

Clauses built on a short-term playbook go stale when the environment changes. Judgments built on more stable demand are fitter to maintain. Amazon retail's three long-term demands supply a sample.

A 2007 *Harvard Business Review* interview recorded Amazon's long-term orientation toward more selection, lower prices, and faster delivery, and used "customers will not, ten years out, ask for slower delivery" to mark its stability (Kirby & Stewart, 2007). The 2008 shareholder letter, filed with the SEC in 2009, wrote the three as low prices, vast selection, and fast, convenient delivery, and explicitly tied the time-stability of the demands to continued investment (Amazon, 2009).

The letter then showed, separately, how investment landed on each of the three: keep lowering prices and offering free shipping; expand selection; improve delivery through Prime and Fulfillment by Amazon. It can prove that the company publicly connected stable demand to continued investment. It cannot prove that each investment was decided by those three sentences alone, and still less that they account for all of Amazon's later success.

At the 2019 re:MARS conference, Amazon's official live record again used low cost and fast delivery to illustrate "what will not change in the next ten years" (Amazon, 2019). These materials record the same set of long-term judgments. They cannot prove that the judgments never changed, and they cannot prove that all of Amazon's resources were allocated by the three demands.

Stable demand and a fixed method are not the same thing. Demand for faster delivery and lower total cost can persist without the delivery network, the technical solution, the specific time window, or the pricing method staying forever the same. What is stable is the direction of value. Methods have to keep iterating with technology, competition, regulation, and feedback. Treat a means as an invariant, and the standard becomes an obstacle. Treat a long-term demand as a short-term slogan, and the organization cannot form continuous investment.

Bezos answers where to build a standard first. Boeing answers what kind of file has not yet become an effective standard. They are not a same-industry experiment, and they cannot prove that a standard is enough for success. They support two steps: choose a judgment worth maintaining for the long term, then make sure it actually enters the floor.

## 8. Eight layers of standards are not eight slogans

A front-line qualification line, a hiring trade-off, and a company mission cannot be mixed into one checklist, and they cannot sit unrelated either. Organizational standards can be split by function into eight layers:

1. **Mission** bounds why the organization exists, and what value it ultimately creates, for whom. It decides which results are worth pursuing, and which impressive numbers have nothing to do with the organization.
2. **Belief** writes the causal hypotheses the organization is prepared to use for the long term, and supplies a path for realizing the mission.
3. **Behavior standards** say by what means the organization may act, and which means cannot be used even if they work in the short term. They draw a boundary around results that cannot be traded away.
4. **Thinking standards** say how evidence is to be handled when a judgment is formed: which angles have to be checked, how a counterexample is faced, and what information is not enough to support a conclusion.
5. **Judgment standards** rank among several feasible options: which value comes first, how risk and return are traded, and under what conditions the option should be dropped.
6. **People-investment standards** answer where scarce people and development resource go, and how to tell whether a people investment is still worth continuing.
7. **Management standards** allocate decision rights, responsibility, and an escalation path: who may make which class of decision independently, and past what boundary it must go to whom.
8. **Learning standards** bring execution results back to the seven layers above: what needs a review, who owns the revision, and how the organization confirms that it is not changing only the action, while leaving unchanged the judgment that produced the action.

The eight layers are not eight independent campaigns. Mission gives the object of value. Belief supplies the long-term hypothesis. Behavior standards limit means. Thinking standards constrain how a conclusion is produced. Judgment standards complete the trade-off. People-investment and management standards put resource and decision rights in the right hands. Learning standards then use real results to repair everything in front.

A new line of business that is expected to be profitable still has to pass, in order, the constraints of mission, long-term belief, behavioral boundaries, and evidence requirements, before it enters a concrete trade-off, people investment, and the allocation of decision rights. After the result returns, the learning layer keeps checking the hypotheses in front.

Mission and belief constitute organizational standards only when they can constrain choices below, give a priority in a conflict, and accept review against results. "Users first," if it cannot say how revenue, safety, and trust are ranked when they collide, is only a wish. "Integrity," if it cannot stop a misleading sales move, has not entered behavior.

The eight layers mark the function of different standards and how they connect. They cannot replace the concrete methods each layer needs.

## 9. AI needs a standard, and it also needs context

A model can infer, ask follow-up questions, and find some contradictions. When the organization has not given a clear judgment standard, the model will call a default formed in training, or infer a ranking from the material in front of it. That fill-in may meet the requirement. It may also conflict with the responsibility the organization bears.

So a standard is an indispensable basis of trade-off and acceptance when an organization assigns work to AI. It is not the whole of the task. A runnable AI task contains at least five kinds of thing.

- **Judgment standard**: the ranking of goals, the qualification line, the evidence required, the boundaries that cannot be crossed, and the stop condition.
- **Context**: the facts, materials, constraints, versions, assumptions, and unknowns this instance of the task involves.
- **Workflow**: what is done first and what next, when a tool is called, and when the work is handed to a person.
- **Tools and permissions**: which systems the AI may read, which actions it can execute, and which writes must be approved.
- **Feedback**: how human acceptance, business results, anomalies, and exceptions return to the next version of the standard.

Stuffing an old SOP whole into a model is not a completed AI conversion. The real conversion extracts the judgment standard, maintains context on its own, turns the steps into a callable workflow, and then makes permissions and feedback explicit.

The British education-software company Arbor Education used that division of labor on complex report support. The team built "conversational procedures" so that AI would ask for missing information, identify the report type, query two internal template libraries, and then guide the user through the report step by step (Zendesk, 2025a). Zendesk's product documentation shows that such procedures are described by the enterprise as business policy and handling method; the system then generates an inspectable procedure graph, which can include questions, conditional judgments, knowledge search, system calls, and human escalation (Zendesk, 2025b). Here, type and escalation boundary are the standard; user answers and the template libraries are context; asking, retrieving, and guiding are workflow.

Arbor reported a 71% automatic-resolution rate on that use case, against an average of about 50% on its other AI use cases. The company also appointed a digital self-service lead to maintain training material, performance metrics, and low-satisfaction feedback. The material was published by Zendesk. The numbers come from an Arbor lead's account. They have not been independently audited, and they cannot prove that an overall drop in tickets was caused by this procedure alone. The case records that handling rules, materials, steps, and escalation conditions were given to AI separately, and then kept under maintenance against results.

A vague requirement lets a model fill in what was never made explicit, and human staff will also fill gaps with experience. Either practice can let a bad standard go undiscovered for a long time. Governance has to expose what was left unspecified: ask when context is insufficient, stop when standards conflict, escalate when permissions are exceeded, and leave a reason when a person changes the work.

Scale will amplify both the virtues of a standard and its gaps. A clear standard can let different people and AIs work stably inside an allowed difference. A wrong standard will let them go wrong in the same way. A correct standard paired with wrong context will still produce a wrong answer. AI has not cancelled the two unifications. It has made "on what scale" and "on which facts" easier to copy in batches, and therefore more in need of separate governance.

The standards cycle still needs a first version of a standard as input. Where that first version comes from, the conditions under which it can migrate, and cheap verification, are the next problem to solve.

## What to Do Monday Morning (principal-leader view)

Pick one repeated piece of work that, in the last month, had the most rework, is about to be expanded, or is about to be handed to AI. Complete three steps:

1. **Separate the standard from the context.** On the left, write the judgment standard: what ranks higher in this work, which boundaries cannot be crossed, and what evidence counts as good. On the right, write the present context: facts already confirmed, the policy version in use, constraints, assumptions, and unknowns. Anything that cannot be placed on either side, mark first. Do not paper it over with a new noun.
2. **Fill in the five items and name an owner.** Check conditions of use, action, acceptance evidence, stop condition, and revision trigger. Write who maintains this standard, where execution feedback lives, and at what time or on what signal it must be reviewed. If a key item cannot be written, shrink the original work into a trial with a scope, a budget, and a stop line. Do not enter scaled execution.
3. **Run a change-of-person and change-of-AI acceptance.** Find someone who did not take part in writing it, and have an AI use the same judgment standard and the same version of context, each completing the work once. Record where the standard was unspecified, the stop condition, and the acceptance evidence. If both sides can only restate the goal, or fill the gaps with their own common sense, the standard is not finished. Return to step one and revise. Do not raise the volume of the task.

At the same time, put a pause rule in place: anyone may report that "this work has no usable standard," and the owner must, inside an agreed window, decide to keep using, revise, trial, or escalate. The pause itself is not a penalty. Hiding a risk and continuing to expand execution needs a record and a handling.
